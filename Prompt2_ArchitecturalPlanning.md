Something I've noticed over time is that if you create something temporary, just to get a feature or model in place before the final version is complete, then if that temporary solution works, it will become a permanent solution. This very much applies to tightly coupled code in games, as there isn't any inherent issue within large files that the computer will complain about or break because of. It will just work, which will make it easy to forget about or ignore, until the time eventually comes when it turns into a massive problem.

The problems that stem from code written in this way are not simple syntax errors or anything like that, instead, it can cause the amount of time required for the debugging process to balloon out of control. Just by the nature of having poorly written code, or extremely large and complex files, debugging become far more difficult. In large files, it can be extremely hard to track down the actual source of a problem, since programs usually don't break at the exact point where the issue lies. In complex or poorly written code, you might discover an issue, which in actuality was holding the whole thing together, and fixing it would cause a cascade of other more dramatic problems to surface.

This is why it's important to always write simple code, separated into smaller, easier to parse files. With smaller and simpler files, it becomes much easier for multiple coders to push their changes without crashing headfirst into each other and needing to resolve hundreds of conflicts at once. When you have many files instead of a single large one, it also becomes much, much easier to pinpoint where errors are occuring in your code. This is also why it's so important to make sure you write your code in an easily understandable way, with comments wherever needed and concise variable names.
